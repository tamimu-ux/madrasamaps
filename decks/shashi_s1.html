Faṣl 5: the two routes of isti'ara (borrowing) in shar'i rulings

Having established in Faṣl 4 that majāz needs a link to the ḥaqīqa, al-Shāshī now defines the two legal routes along which one word may be 'borrowed' for another in the rulings of the Sharī'a. Route 1 links a cause ('illa) with its effect (ḥukm): the borrowing is valid in both directions. Route 2 links a purely-instrumental cause (sabab maḥḍ) with the ruling: the borrowing is valid in one direction only, from the asl (the cause-word) to the far' (the effect-word). The examples are all fiqh: 'malaktu/ishtaraytu', 'ḥarrartuki' for ṭalāq, and nikāḥ by the words of hiba/tamlīk/bay'. The section closes with the Ṣāḥibayn's condition of imkān al-ḥaqīqa and how the book answers it.
By how many routes does isti'ara operate in the rulings of the Sharia?
اعْلَم أَن الِاسْتِعَارَة فِي أَحْكَام الشَّرْع مطردَة بطريقين
By two: (1) a link between an 'illa and its ruling; (2) a link between a sabab maḥḍ and the ruling.tap to reveal
What is the effect of route 1 (illa-hukm link)?
فَالْأول مِنْهُمَا يُوجب صِحَة الِاسْتِعَارَة من الطَّرفَيْنِ
Borrowing is valid from either side: the cause-word can stand for the effect and the effect-word for the cause.tap to reveal
If someone says 'If I buy a slave he is free', buys half, sells it, then buys the other half, is the second half freed? And what if he intended 'own' by 'buy' (or vice versa)?
وَلَو عَنى بِالْملكِ الشِّرَاء أَو بِالشِّرَاءِ الْملك صحت نِيَّته بطرِيق الْمجَاز لِأَن الشِّرَاء عِلّة الْملك وَالْملك حكمه فعمت الِاسْتِعَارَة بَين الْعلَّة والمعلول من الطَّرفَيْنِ
With 'ishtaraytu' the second half is freed (purchase was actually completed in that half); and either word may validly be intended for the other by niyya, as majāz.tap to reveal

Faṣl 6: the explicit (ṣarīḥ) and the allusive (kināya) wording

This faṣl divides wording by how plainly the speaker's meaning shows. Ṣarīḥ is wording whose intended meaning is manifest ('bi'tu', 'ishtaraytu'): its ruling takes effect in any grammatical form (report, description, vocative) and needs no niyya. Kināya is wording whose meaning is hidden: its ruling needs a niyya or a contextual indication (dalālat al-ḥāl) to remove hesitation, so it cannot support ḥudūd. The main fiqh applications are talāq/'itq wording, tayammum (the Ḥanafī reading of 'li-yuṭahhirakum' as a ṣarīḥ text vs the Shāfiʿī's two reports), and the rejection of ḥadd on kināya admissions or answers like 'ṣadaqta'.
What is ṣarīḥ?
الصَّرِيح لفظ يكون المُرَاد بِهِ ظَاهرا كَقَوْلِه بِعْت واشتريت وَأَمْثَاله
Wording whose intended meaning is manifest, like 'I sold', 'I bought'.tap to reveal
What is the ruling of ṣarīḥ?
وَحكمه أَنه يُوجب ثُبُوت مَعْنَاهُ بِأَيّ طَرِيق كَانَ من إِخْبَار أَو نعت أَو نِدَاء
It establishes its meaning in whatever way it occurs: as a report, a description, or a vocative.tap to reveal
Does ṣarīḥ need a niyya?
وَمن حكمه أَنه يَسْتَغْنِي عَن النِّيَّة
No, it dispenses with niyya.tap to reveal

Faṣl 7: the four clear categories (ẓāhir, naṣṣ, mufassar, muḥkam) and their four opposites (khafī, mushkil, mujmal, mutashābih)

Al-Shāshī ranks wording by the degree of clarity of the intended meaning. In ascending strength: ẓāhir (clear on hearing), naṣṣ (clearer because the speech was driven for it), mufassar (explained by the speaker so that ta'wīl and takhṣīṣ are shut), muḥkam (so strong that it cannot be contradicted or abrogated). Their opposites in ascending obscurity are khafī (hidden by an external factor), mushkil (more hidden), mujmal (can be known only from the speaker's explanation) and mutashābih (hidden beyond hope in this life). Each pair is tied to a ruling: the stronger must be preferred over the weaker when they conflict, and the obscure categories require investigation (khafī, mushkil) or acceptance until bayān (mujmal, mutashābih). The examples are Qurʾānic verses and the oath/iqrār/talāq rulings.
How is 'wa-aḥalla Allāhu al-bay' wa ḥarrama al-ribā' both naṣṣ and ẓāhir?
فالآية سيقت لبَيَان التَّفْرِقَة بَين البيع والربا ردا لما ادَّعَاهُ الْكفَّار من التَّسْوِيَة بَينهمَا
The verse was driven to distinguish bay' from ribā (refuting the claim of equality), so it is naṣṣ in the distinction, and ẓāhir in the lawfulness of sale and prohibition of ribā.tap to reveal
In 'fankiḥū mā ṭāba lakum min al-nisā' mathnā wa thulātha wa rubā'' what is naṣṣ and what is ẓāhir?
سيق الْكَلَام لبَيَان الْعدَد وَقد علم الْإِطْلَاق وَالْإِجَازَة بِنَفس السماع فَصَارَ ذَلِك ظَاهرا فِي حق الْإِطْلَاق نصا فِي بَيَان الْعدَد
Naṣṣ in the number (up to four), ẓāhir in the permission to marry.tap to reveal
In 'lā junāḥa 'alaykum in ṭallaqtum al-nisā' mā lam tamassūhunna aw tafriḍū lahunna farīḍa' what is naṣṣ, ẓāhir and ishāra?
نَص فِي حكم من لم يسم لَهَا الْمهْر وَظَاهر فِي استبداد الزَّوْج بِالطَّلَاق وَإِشَارَة إِلَى أَن النِّكَاح بِدُونِ ذكر الْمهْر يَصح
Naṣṣ in the ruling for a woman for whom no mahr was named; ẓāhir in the husband's sole power of divorce; ishāra that nikāḥ without mentioning the mahr is valid.tap to reveal

Faṣl 8: the five things by which the literal meaning (ḥaqīqa) of a word is abandoned

Words establish rulings because they indicate the speaker's intended meaning. When something shows that the literal meaning is not intended, the ḥaqīqa is abandoned. Al-Shāshī gives five such indications: (1) 'urf (common usage), (2) an indication within the speech itself, (3) the context (siyāq) of the speech, (4) an indication from the speaker (his wisdom, circumstances or the occasion of his words), and (5) the subject-matter (maḥall) which cannot accept the literal meaning. The fiqh examples are oaths, wakāla, amān to a ḥarbī, emancipation of a mukātab/mudabbar, and the nikāḥ of a free woman by the words of bay'/hiba. Abandoning the ḥaqīqa does not always mean going to majāz: it may leave a restricted ḥaqīqa (as in takhṣīṣ of a general term).
How many things abandon the literal meaning of words and what is the reason for abandonment?
وَذَلِكَ لِأَن ثُبُوت الْأَحْكَام بالألفاظ إِنَّمَا كَانَ لدلَالَة اللَّفْظ على الْمَعْنى المُرَاد للمتكلم
Five: 'urf, indication within the speech, context, indication from the speaker, and the object of the speech.tap to reveal
What is the first kind: 'urf?
فَإِذا كَانَ الْمَعْنى متعارفا بَين النَّاس كَانَ ذَلِك الْمَعْنى الْمُتَعَارف دَلِيلا على أَنه هُوَ المُرَاد بِهِ ظَاهرا فيترتب عَلَيْهِ الحكم
When a meaning is common among people, that meaning is the evidence of what is intended, so the ruling is built on it.tap to reveal
Oath not to eat eggs: does eating a sparrow's or pigeon's egg break it?
لَو حلف لَا يَأْكُل بيضًا كَانَ ذَلِك على الْمُتَعَارف فَلَا يَحْنَث بتناول بيض العصفور والحمامة
No, the oath refers to what is customary.tap to reveal

Faṣl 9: what attaches to texts: 'ibārat al-naṣṣ, ishārat al-naṣṣ, dalālat al-naṣṣ, iqtiḍā' al-naṣṣ

Al-Shāshī now shows four ways a text yields rulings. 'Ibārat al-naṣṣ is the meaning for which the speech was driven and which was intended. Ishārat al-naṣṣ is a meaning established by the very structure of the text without addition, not apparent in every respect and not what the speech was driven for (e.g. the muhājirūn being called 'poor' shows that the kuffār's seizure of Muslim property gives them ownership; the permission of mubāshara until dawn shows janāba does not break the fast). Dalālat al-naṣṣ is a meaning grasped linguistically (not by ijtihād) as the 'illa of a stated ruling, so the ruling extends to everything with that 'illa (uff to parents -> beating, kaffāra for eating via jimā'). Iqtiḍā' al-naṣṣ is an addition without which the text cannot be sound; it is established by necessity and so is limited to the extent of necessity. Their strength order: 'ibāra, ishāra, dalāla, iqtiḍā'.
What are the four 'mutaʿalliqāt al-nuṣūṣ'?
نعني بهَا عبارَة النَّص وإشارته ودلالته واقتضاءه
'Ibāra, ishāra, dalāla, iqtiḍā'.tap to reveal
What is 'ibārat al-naṣṣ?
فَأَما عبارَة النَّص فَهُوَ مَا سيق الْكَلَام لأَجله وَأُرِيد بِهِ قصدا
The meaning for which the speech was driven and which was deliberately intended.tap to reveal
What is ishārat al-naṣṣ?
وَأما إِشَارَة النَّص فَهِيَ مَا ثَبت بنظم النَّص ... من غير زِيَادَة وَهُوَ غير ظَاهر من كل وَجه وَلَا سيق الْكَلَام لأَجله
What is established by the structure of the text, without addition, not apparent in every respect, and not what the speech was driven for.tap to reveal

1A man says 'tazawwajtu fulāna shahran bi-kadhā': is it nikāḥ?
It is mut'a and not nikāḥ.
2Does janāba at dawn invalidate the fast?
No, janāba does not contradict the fast.
3Someone says 'free your slave on my behalf for a thousand dirhams' and he says 'I freed': who is the freer, and is the thousand due?
The emancipation takes effect on behalf of the one who commanded and he owes the thousand; if he intended an expiation, it counts as he intended.
4What is the ruling of kināya and its status relative to majāz?
Majāz before it becomes common usage is like kināya. The ruling of kināya is established by it only when a niyya is present or by dalālat al-ḥāl (context).
5Oath not to eat meat: does fish or locust meat break it? Pork or human flesh?
Fish and locust: no breach. Pork or human flesh: he breaks it.
6What is the fourth kind and its example in the Qur'an?
An indication from the speaker. In 'let him disbelieve', the literal meaning (command) is abandoned.
7In 'lā junāḥa 'alaykum in ṭallaqtum al-nisā' mā lam tamassūhunna aw tafriḍū lahunna farīḍa' what is naṣṣ, ẓāhir and ishāra?
Naṣṣ in the ruling for a woman for whom no mahr was named; ẓāhir in the husband's sole power of divorce; ishāra that nikāḥ without mentioning the mahr is valid.
8A man says to his wife 'harrartuki' (I have freed you) intending talaq: does it work? Is it raji'?
Yes, it is valid, and the divorce is bā'in (irrevocable), not raj'ī.
9By how many routes does isti'ara operate in the rulings of the Sharia?
